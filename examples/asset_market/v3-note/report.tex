\begin{abstract}
The new market completed all 30 rounds and 360 valid trader decisions.
Its first transaction was at \$22; the price rose to \$29.50, more than twice
the \$14 fundamental value, before the last trade at \$23.75. Turnover was
107 shares across 17 trading rounds. This is a rising, persistently overpriced
market with a late decline, although that decline rests on a single-share
trade. Three traders ultimately held all 48 shares and finished far below a
passive holding benchmark. The report visualizes every decision and fill,
compares the three full sessions, and documents one failed response and its
recovery. The appendix prints the complete experiment and core implementation,
with new or modified code highlighted relative to the previous report.
\end{abstract}
\section{Design and benchmark}
Each trader starts with \$100 and four shares. Every round collects sealed
orders, clears a uniform-price call market, credits 5\% interest to cash,
and pays a common per-share dividend of \$0.40 or \$1 with equal probability.
After round 30's income, shares redeem for \$14. Borrowing and short selling
are prohibited. With $n=31-t$ income payments remaining before round $t$,
\[
 V_t=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{0.70}{1.05^k}+\frac{14}{1.05^n}=14.
\]
The return available on cash matters: adding dividends to redemption without
discounting overvalues the asset. Settlement rounds monetary amounts to cents.

Two traders receive each archetype: Rational Arbitrageur (RA), Momentum Chaser
(MC), Bubble Timer (BT), Precommitter (PC), Noise Trader (NT), and Overconfident
Contrarian (OC). Our exploratory \texttt{speculative-v1} prompts encourage
resale beliefs, speculative participation, or exit plans by type. The supplied
\emph{Building Traders} slides motivate the design \cite{ngo}; these are our
prompts, not the authors' original prompts or a numerical replication.

All three full sessions use the same economic parameters, seats, persona
texts, model settings, seed 140926, and realized dividends, with fresh model
answers. V3 uses GPT-5 mini, medium reasoning, temperature 1, and an 8,000-token
limit. All 30 rounds were specified in advance. The question describes the
contract but does not supply the valuation calculation or an opening quote.

The first clearing matched trader 10's four-share ask at \$22 with three

then rose 34.1\% from first trade to peak. All 17 transaction prices exceeded

The final decline deserves care. In round 29, trader 00 bid \$31.50 for one
share and trader 11 offered one at \$16. The midpoint rule set
$(31.50+16)/2=\$23.75$. A lower print therefore need not imply a broad downward
revision in buyers' beliefs. V3 exhibits an inflated price path and a late
transaction-price decline; evidence for a broad market crash is limited.

\section{Every trader decision and executed fill}
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/decisions.pdf}
\caption{All 360 decisions, indexed by trader and round. Background colors
show intended side. Numbers show actual signed fills: positive for purchases,
negative for sales. A colored cell without a number is an unfilled order;
a hold cell is a deliberate hold. Each column's signed fills sum to zero.}
\end{figure}
The 360 admitted decisions comprise 147 buy orders, 41 sell orders, and 172
holds. No orders were rejected or reduced by the admission cap in this run.
Admission does not imply execution: buy and sell limits must cross, and
available opposite-side quantity may be smaller than requested quantity.
Total turnover counts shares once per matched transaction, not twice for
buyer and seller.

The market sorts bids from high to low and asks from low to high, applies
seeded priority within equal prices, and identifies crossing quantity.
All accepted matches execute at one price: the midpoint of the marginal
matched bid and ask, rounded to cents. Unfilled orders expire after the round.
The market then credits interest, pays dividends, and advances the public tape
and private account histories. Traders see the tape and their own accounts;
they do not see other traders' sealed orders before choosing.

\subsection*{Economic reasoning remains imperfect}
Trader 05's round-5 rationale valued the asset by adding 26 expected dividends
of \$0.70 to the \$14 redemption, obtaining \$32.20. That omits discounting at
the available 5\% cash return; the corresponding benchmark is \$14. By round
25, trader 00 cited the previous \$29 transaction and fear of missing a
continuing rally, submitted a \$30 bid for two shares, and bought at \$29.50.
These are elicited explanations, not independent measurements of latent beliefs.

The question explicitly requests \$14 forecasts for dates beyond round 30.
Nevertheless, 17 of 204 such forecast entries (8.3\%) differ from \$14.
Structurally valid answers can therefore contain economic mistakes. The
bubble-like outcome may combine resale expectations, persona framing, and
misunderstanding; this single session cannot separate those explanations.
The exact four discussed decisions are included in
\path{data/discussed-decisions.json}.

\section{Who held the asset, and who finished ahead?}
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/wealth.pdf}
\caption{Terminal cash relative to never trading. The passive account keeps
its initial four shares, receives the same realized dividends and cash
interest, and redeems at the end, finishing with \$648.69. Actual terminal
cash includes redemption; no shares remain afterward.}
\end{figure}
Traders 00 (MC), 05 (OC), and 08 (MC) held 17, 16, and 15 shares immediately
before redemption: all 48 outstanding shares. They finished with \$268.66,
\$283.49, and \$340.06, respectively. Each lost more than \$300 relative to
passive holding. The other nine traders held no shares at redemption and
finished above that benchmark.

This comparison includes the consequences of cash interest, not simply
purchase price minus redemption. Selling earlier transfers shares away and
leaves cash earning interest; buying at inflated prices commits cash that
would otherwise compound. Every account starts with the same endowment,
so the common passive benchmark is a useful accounting comparison.

Total terminal cash is \$7,784.35, compared with \$7,784.28 for twelve passive
accounts. The seven-cent difference reflects per-account rounding. The large
individual gains and losses primarily redistribute terminal wealth; they do
not represent additional aggregate asset value.

These outcomes describe twelve particular model-generated histories. With
only two traders per archetype in one market, there is no reliable estimate
of an archetype effect. In particular, the two OC traders finished on opposite
sides of the passive benchmark. Full terminal accounts appear in
Appendix~\ref{app:accounts}.

\section{What changed in the code}
The new authoring layer replaces loosely specified settings with validated
objects. The default market economics and serialized version-1 machine
remain compatible with the earlier experiment. Three layers have distinct jobs:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Question and response contract.} \texttt{DecisionSchema} builds
an EDSL \texttt{QuestionDict}, including named forecast fields. It defines
what each trader is asked and the expected answer fields.
\item \textbf{Workflow and execution plan.} The workflow schedules trader
work followed by settlement for 30 rounds. The execution plan assigns LLM
execution to traders and scripted execution to the exchange. The exchange's
placeholder answer is \texttt{clear}; its state-changing effect calls
\texttt{market.settle()}.
\item \textbf{Shared-state machine.} Registered native handlers admit orders
and perform settlement. The experiment serializes their declarative machine
specification and operation names, not arbitrary executable Python source.
Loading still requires a compatible EDSL runtime with those handlers installed.
\end{enumerate}
Each ordinary round constructs \emph{one} EDSL job containing twelve paired
agent/scenario interviews. \texttt{zip\_assign(over=("agents", "scenarios"))}
pairs each trader with its private view, avoiding a 12-by-12 cross product.
Rounds depend on prior settlement and therefore advance sequentially.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}\toprule
Configuration & Settings available for controlled variation\\\midrule
\texttt{CallMarketRules} & Number of rounds; homogeneous cash/share endowment;
midpoint, bid, or ask pricing; cash interest; dividend distribution; income
order; redemption; buy cap; initial reference; forecast keys.\\
\texttt{TraderTreatment} & Archetype composition; assignment seed; model and
service; temperature, reasoning effort and token limit; forecast horizons.\\
Market seed & Seeded randomness for dividends and order priority, separated
internally by purpose.\\\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Unknown fields, invalid numeric values, malformed probability vectors, and
inconsistent settings fail during construction. Use \texttt{with\_changes(...)}
for validated edits; Pydantic's \texttt{model\_copy(update=...)} bypasses
validation. Rules also generate economic instructions and compute the valuation
benchmark from the configured payment order, including zero-interest variants.

\texttt{OrderIntent}, \texttt{TraderDecision}, \texttt{AcceptedOrder},
\texttt{TradeFill}, \texttt{Account}, and \texttt{RoundOutcome} validate runtime
records. Generic maps still exist in the serialized DSL. Semantic finance
reasoning and beyond-terminal forecast compliance are not automatically enforced.
No borrowing/shorting, order expiration, cent rounding, seeded tie priority,
and final redemption timing remain fixed policies. Heterogeneous endowments,
information disclosure, and independent dividend/tie seed overrides are not
implemented as treatment settings.

\section{Execution, recovery, and limits}
Successful twelve-interview jobs averaged \textbf{35.1 seconds}. This excludes
the failed final batch and its one-interview retry. There were 31 submissions:
30 planned round jobs and one retry. The archive has 30 batch-completion
records because the failed original final batch did not reach that logging
step. Wall time including diagnosis and recovery was \textbf{31.8 minutes}.
Recorded model cost was \textbf{\$1.2877}; the failed result has no usage data,
so this may understate actual billed cost.

\subsection*{One incomplete response, followed by a guarded recovery}
The initial round-30 job yielded eleven valid decisions and one null decision
for trader 05. The null record includes no raw model response or usage,
so its underlying cause is unknown. The backend correctly rejected it as
\texttt{decision must be a map}. However, the workflow had already recorded
an immutable submission intent and left that item in \texttt{committing}.
At that point, 359 valid decisions and 29 settlements were preserved.

The executor now checks that every required answer is present and non-null
\emph{before} creating a submission intent. The appendix highlights this
small guard within the complete executor. Two targeted regression tests passed:
null results do not create an immutable intent, and a partially successful
batch can retry the unfinished work.

The already-stuck item required a separate, one-time database repair. The
script backed up both databases, verified the exact null intent and its single
unapplied effect, and confirmed that no corresponding market event existed.
It archived and removed that invalid intent, then made only the failed item
retryable. A one-trader EDSL job returned a valid decision; normal round-30
settlement then redeemed all 48 shares. This was a guarded intervention,
not an existing general-purpose recovery API. Its source is preserved in the
bundle for audit, not as a routine rerun command.

\subsection*{What the completed audit establishes}
The final audit passed: 360 decisions, 30 settlements, 390 successful attempts
and one failed attempt; all private views and prompts verified; all shares
redeemed. It also verified that the 359 decisions and 29 settlements completed
before recovery were unchanged. The raw archive retains all 361 result records;
the replay file contains the 360 valid decisions. Source hashes distinguish
the executor used initially from the fixed executor used for the retry.

The audit establishes execution consistency, not correct economic reasoning.
V3 supports the narrower finding that this configuration can generate a
sustained price inflation with a late decline. Repeated sessions and controlled
prompt/understanding treatments would be needed to distinguish behavioral
mechanisms from valuation errors and to estimate how often bubbles emerge.
